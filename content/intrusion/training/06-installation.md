# Module 6: Installation

**You'll be able to:** survey a site, design a system, place every device correctly, and install the panel, keypads, sirens, and communicator.

---

## Lesson 6.1: Site survey

Walk the whole property with the customer before you design anything.

**Ask the customer**
- Who lives or works here? Pets (weight, do they climb)? Kids? Elderly?
- Which door do you use most?
- What are you most worried about? (Break-in while away, while sleeping, a specific valuables room, a detached garage)
- Any previous break-ins or false alarm history?
- Do you want smoke, CO, water, or cameras later?

**Check the building**
- Every exterior door, window, and basement or crawlspace access
- Construction: drywall, plaster, block, finished vs unfinished basement and attic (this decides wired vs wireless)
- Panel location options near an outlet
- Cell signal strength at the panel location
- Existing system or wiring you can reuse
- Local alarm permit requirements (see Module 8)

---

## Lesson 6.2: Designing the system

**Layers of protection**
1. **Perimeter:** contacts on exterior doors and accessible windows; glass break where glass can be broken.
2. **Interior:** motion detectors covering traffic paths (main hallway, stairs, rooms with valuables).
3. **Life safety:** smoke, heat, CO if part of the job.
4. **Annunciation and communication:** keypads, sirens, communicator.

**Design rules of thumb**
- Every exterior door gets a contact.
- Ground-floor and accessible windows get contacts or glass break coverage.
- Place at least one motion so an intruder can't get from any ground-floor opening to the bedrooms or valuables without crossing it.
- Primary keypad within reach of the main entry door; second keypad by the master bedroom or garage entry as needed.
- Group devices into zones so the central station report tells police where to go.

Write a **zone list** before you start: zone number, name, device type, zone type, wired or wireless.

## Lesson 6.3: Device placement quick rules

| Device | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Door contact | Mount on the latch side at the top of the door | Mount on the hinge side (smallest gap movement) |
| Window contact | Mount on the moving sash | Leave windows that open with no contact |
| PIR | Mount at the rated height, aimed across traffic | Aim at windows, heat sources, vents, fans |
| Glass break | Line of sight to the glass, within rated range | Mount on the same wall as the glass if the manufacturer prohibits it, or behind heavy drapes |
| Keypad | Inside, near entry, about 4 to 5 ft high | Visible through a window from outside |
| Interior siren | Central location, out of easy reach | Right next to the keypad where it's easily silenced |
| Exterior siren/strobe | High, hard to reach, visible from the street | Within easy reach |

## Lesson 6.4: Mounting the panel and power

1. Mount the can on a solid surface at a comfortable working height, with room around it for wiring.
2. Bring all cables in neatly through knockouts. Use grommets or bushings.
3. Connect earth ground per the manual.
4. Run the transformer wire. Use the transformer the manual specifies, plugged into a non-switched outlet. Secure it with its retaining screw if it has one.
5. Land all zones, bus devices, and outputs before applying power.
6. **Power-up order:** connect the battery first or AC first as the manual says. Many manuals say AC first, then battery. Follow your panel's instructions.
7. Verify voltage at aux and bell outputs under load.

---

## Lesson 6.5: Keypads and sirens

- Address each keypad before or after landing it, as the manual says. Duplicate addresses cause bus trouble.
- Confirm keypad backlight and display at the farthest keypad; low voltage there means a voltage drop problem.
- Siren wiring is polarity-sensitive on most sirens: positive to bell +, negative to bell −.
- Some panels supervise the bell circuit and need an EOL resistor on it.

## Lesson 6.6: Communicators

| Path | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Cellular** | Works when phone/internet lines are cut; most common today | Needs a usable signal; network sunsets require upgrades |
| **IP (internet)** | Fast, low cost | Fails if the router or internet is down or the customer changes equipment |
| **Dual-path (cell + IP)** | Redundant; often required for commercial | More cost |
| **POTS phone line** | Legacy | Copper landlines are being retired in many areas |

**Install steps**
- Check signal strength at the panel location before you mount. Use the communicator's own signal readout.
- If signal is weak, use a remote antenna or relocate the communicator (within the manufacturer's limits).
- Activate the communicator on the provider's portal and link it to the central station account.
- Send test signals and confirm with the central station that they arrived with the right account and zone.

## Module 6 quiz

1. Name four questions to ask the customer during the survey.
2. What are the three layers of intrusion protection?
3. Which side of a door should a contact go on?
4. Why shouldn't the keypad be visible through a window?
5. What should you check before mounting a cellular communicator?
6. Why is dual-path communication more reliable?
7. Why does a duplicate keypad address cause problems?

**Answer key:** 1) Any four from Lesson 6.1. 2) Perimeter, interior, annunciation/communication (life safety as an add-on). 3) The latch side. 4) An intruder could see codes being entered or system status. 5) Signal strength at that location. 6) If one path fails, the other still sends the signal. 7) The panel can't tell the two keypads apart, causing communication errors.
