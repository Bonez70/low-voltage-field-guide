# Module 7: Installation

**You'll be able to:** survey a site, mount cameras where they'll actually see what the customer needs, aim and set up the image, keep outdoor cameras dry, and commission and hand off the system.

---

## Lesson 7.1: Site survey and design

Walk the site with the customer and answer these for every camera:

1. **What must it see, and at what level?** (Detect, observe, recognize, identify; Lesson 1.1.)
2. **How far is the target,** and how wide is the area? Work out pixel density with the **Field of View calculator**.
3. **What's the light** day and night? Bright windows, sun, headlights, dark corners.
4. **Where does the cable go,** and how long is the run? Over 100 m for Ethernet needs a plan (Lesson 5.1).
5. **What's it mounted to,** and can you reach it with a ladder or does it need a lift?

**Write it down:** a floor plan with every camera, its view, mounting height, and model. Many manufacturers have free design tools that draw the field of view on the plan. Photos of each location with the intended view save arguments later.

**Ask about the network** before you arrive: who owns the switches, which VLAN, which IPs, and whether you can install your own switch.

> **Field tip (David to add):** the question you always ask on a survey that customers don't think of.

## Lesson 7.2: Mounting

- **Height for faces:** cameras meant to identify people at doors are commonly mounted **8 to 10 ft** high, with the vertical angle down to the face kept small (under about 15 to 30°). Mounted at 14 ft looking straight down, you get the tops of hats. [VERIFY:mount-height]
- **Height for overviews:** 10 to 14 ft or higher, out of easy reach.
- **Into structure.** Use the mount the manufacturer made for the surface: wall, pendant, corner, pole, or junction box adapter. Anchors rated for the weight; PTZs and heavy multi-sensors get blocking or a structural mount. [VERIFY:safety-mount]
- **Back boxes or junction boxes** hide the cable and the connection, protect it from weather and tampering, and keep the cable from being cut at the camera.
- **Plan for service:** you'll be back to clean, refocus, and replace. Put cameras where a ladder can reach when you can.

## Lesson 7.3: Aim, focus, and image setup

**Aim:**

- Fill the view with what matters; keep sky, ceilings, and walls to a minimum. Sky also fools exposure, making the ground dark.
- Keep the horizon level.
- Don't aim into the sun at sunrise or sunset, or at headlights.

**Focus** with the IR on, at night if you can, or with IR forced on. Many lenses shift focus between visible and infrared light; a camera focused only in daylight is soft at night. Motorized lenses have an auto-focus button; use it in night mode too.

**Image settings worth setting on every camera:**

- **Shutter cap** around 1/60 to 1/120 s for people (faster for vehicles).
- **WDR** on for cameras facing doors and windows.
- **IR** set to smart or adaptive IR so close faces don't white out.
- **Day/night switch** threshold tuned so it doesn't flip back and forth at dusk.
- **Privacy masks** over anything the camera mustn't see: neighbors' windows, keypads, restroom doorways (Lesson 8.4).

**Check it on the recorder,** not just the camera's web page: play back a recording of someone walking through, at night, and zoom in on the face.

---

## Lesson 7.4: Weatherproofing outdoor cameras

- **Junction box** behind or under every outdoor camera, sealed, so the RJ45 or BNC connection lives inside.
- **Waterproof RJ45 cap** (supplied with most outdoor cameras) assembled fully, with the gasket in.
- **Cable enters from below,** with a drip loop. Seal the wall penetration.
- **Dome bubbles:** don't touch the inside, keep the foam or rubber IR skirt seated against the bubble, and remove the protective film last.
- **Spiders** love warm, IR-lit cameras. Webs trigger motion and glow white at night. Spray-on deterrents and regular cleaning help.

## Lesson 7.5: Commissioning and handoff

Before you call it done:

- [ ] Every camera recording, on the right schedule, at the right resolution and frame rate.
- [ ] Day and night views checked, with someone walking through, on playback.
- [ ] Time correct on the recorder and every camera, synced to one time source. [VERIFY:time-sync]
- [ ] Drives healthy and the recorder showing the expected days of retention (check again after a few weeks).
- [ ] Default passwords changed; user accounts made for the customer (Module 8).
- [ ] Alerts set for drive failure and camera loss, sent to someone who'll act.
- [ ] Remote viewing working on the customer's phone, through the cloud or VPN, not port forwarding.
- [ ] Customer trained to find and export video (Lesson 8.5).
- [ ] As-built: floor plan, IP schedule, passwords handed over securely, and the camera views captured.

Reference: *Commissioning checklist* has the full list.

## Module 7 quiz

1. What five things do you answer for every camera on a survey?
2. Why mount door cameras around 8 to 10 ft instead of high in the corner?
3. Why focus with the IR on?
4. Why does a lot of sky in the view make the ground dark?
5. Where does the RJ45 connection live on an outdoor camera?
6. Why do spiders cause night problems?
7. What's the last check you do before handing over remote viewing?

**Answer key:** 1) What it must see and at what level, distance and width, light day and night, cable path and length, and what it mounts to and how you reach it. 2) To keep the angle to faces small so you see faces, not hats. 3) Many lenses shift focus under infrared, so a daylight focus is soft at night. 4) The camera exposes for the bright sky. 5) Inside a sealed junction box or back box. 6) Webs reflect IR and glow white, and their movement triggers motion recording. 7) That it works on the customer's phone through the cloud or VPN, without port forwarding.
